**Simple Summary**
A Red Herring intentionally derails an argument by introducing a side issue that is completely irrelevant to the topic at hand. 
An Appeal to Emotion relies on manipulating audience feelings (fear, sympathy, anger) instead of providing sound logic or factual evidence.

**Core Structure & Mechanics**
* **Red Herring (*Ignoratio Elenchi*):**
  * **Mechanism:** Topic $A$ is under discussion $\rightarrow$ Introduce irrelevant Topic $B$ $\rightarrow$ Topic $A$ is abandoned.
  * **Key Red Flag:** Abrupt shift in focus toward a tangentially related issue that distracts from answering the main question.
* **Appeal to Emotion (*Argumentum ad Passiones*):**
  * **Mechanism:** Premise $P$ relies on provoking Emotion $E$ $\rightarrow$ Conclude Claim $C$ is true/false based on $E$.
  * **Key Red Flag:** Visceral, dramatic, or guilt-inducing rhetoric that substitutes for concrete data or empirical facts.

**Key Thinkers & Sources**
* **Associated Thinkers:** [[Aristotle]] (Categorized irrelevance traps in *Sophistical Refutations*), [[John Locke]] (Identified emotional appeals)
* **Primary Text:** *[[With Good Reason: An Introduction to Informal Fallacies]]* by [[S. Morris Engel]]

**Connections & Links**

* **Apologetics Application:** Identifying when critics use dramatic stories or outrage to bypass historical or text-critical arguments ([[Appeal to Emotion]]) or shift the focus away from the core debate onto unrelated cultural grievances ([[Red Herring]]).

**Common Misconceptions**
* Including emotional language or genuine passion in an argument is not a fallacy, provided the argument itself is grounded in valid evidence.
* Changing the subject is not a Red Herring if both parties intentionally agree to pivot to a new topic for broader context.
