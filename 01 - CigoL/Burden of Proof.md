**Simple Summary**

The burden of proof is the obligation to provide evidence or argument for a claim. In logic and rational discourse, the person who makes a positive or extraordinary claim generally carries the burden of supporting it — not the person who doubts it. Shifting the burden of proof onto the skeptic is a fallacy, and it is closely related to the Appeal to Ignorance.

**Core Structure & Mechanics**

* **Rule / Mechanism:**
  * **Default presumption:** In the absence of evidence, the default position is not to accept the claim but to withhold judgment. "No evidence has been offered" is not itself evidence against the claim.
  * **Who bears the burden:** The person asserting $P$ bears the burden of supporting $P$. The person denying $P$ does not need to prove $\neg P$ unless they are themselves making a positive counterclaim.
  * **Extraordinary claims:** The more a claim departs from ordinary experience or established knowledge, the heavier the burden of proof. This is a heuristic, not a strict rule — "extraordinary" means "extraordinary evidence would be needed," not "impossible."
  * **Burden can shift:** In a back-and-forth, the burden can shift if one side offers evidence and the other side offers a competing explanation that needs support. But the initial default does not flip just because someone asserts something.

* **Formal Notation (Schematic):**
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{Claimant asserts } P &\implies \text{Burden on Claimant to provide evidence/argument for } P. \\
  \text{Skeptic says "No evidence for } P\text{"} &\implies \text{Not a claim that } \neg P \text{ is true; just a refusal to grant } P. \\
  \text{Skeptic asserts } \neg P &\implies \text{Now Skeptic bears a burden for } \neg P \text{ (if they want others to accept it).}
  \end{aligned}
  $$

* **Classic Example (Burden correctly assigned):**
  Claim: "There is a teapot orbiting the Sun between Earth and Mars." The claimant bears the burden. The skeptic is fully rational to say "I am not convinced" without having to prove the teapot does not exist.

* **Classic Example (Burden shifted — Appeal to Ignorance adjacent):**
  Claim: "You can't prove God doesn't exist, therefore God exists." This shifts the burden: the skeptic's inability to disprove does not constitute proof. (This is the Appeal to Ignorance — see [[Informal Fallacies - Genetic Fallacy and Appeal to Ignorance]].)

* **Classic Example (Burden correctly engaged):**
  Claim: "The resurrection of Jesus is the best explanation of the empty tomb, the eyewitness reports, and the rise of the early church." The claimant here offers a positive case (evidence + inference to the best explanation) rather than merely demanding the skeptic disprove it. That puts the debate on the merits, not on a burden trick.

* **Theological Application:** Burden of proof matters enormously in apologetics and philosophy of religion. A theist who claims "God exists" bears a burden to offer reasons; an atheist who claims "God does not exist" (as a positive metaphysical claim) bears a burden too. A skeptic who merely withholds belief without asserting disbelief does not bear the same burden. Sorting out which position is actually being asserted — theism, atheism, or agnosticism — is often the first step in a productive conversation. This also matters when evaluating miracle claims, religious experience arguments, and the design argument.

**Common Misconceptions**

* "Burden of proof is always on the believer" is too crude. The burden is on whoever makes a positive assertion that requires support — including positive atheism or any other affirmative metaphysical claim.
* Withholding judgment is not the same as disproving. "I am not convinced" and "I have disproven it" are different epistemic states with different burdens.
* A heavy burden on an extraordinary claim does not mean the claim is impossible — it means the evidence required to rationally accept it is high. A well-supported extraordinary claim can still be rationally accepted.
* Having the burden of proof does not make a claim false or even unlikely — it just means the claimant has work to do before the claim should be accepted by a neutral audience.
